Who keeps the record?

The Ledger

All of finance is a notebook of who owes whom.

A ledger is simply a record of transactions. Your bank passbook is a ledger. The Kirana shop's credit notebook is a ledger. When you pay ₹500 by UPI, nothing physical moves. Two ledgers change: your bank reduces your balance and your friend's bank increases theirs.

Today, almost every ledger is centralised. One institution keeps the master copy, and everyone trusts it. That works well, until the keeper makes a mistake, gets hacked, goes offline, or quietly changes an entry. In 2008, you saw what happens when trust in the record keepers breaks.

A distributed ledger takes a different approach. Instead of one master copy, many computers keep identical copies and agree on every change together. No single keeper, no single point of failure.

Attack the centre and see who survives

Left: one bank keeps the only master copy. Right: every computer keeps a full copy.

Key term

Ledger

A record of transactions: who paid whom, how much, and when. Banking is built on ledgers.

Key term

Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT)

A ledger copied across many computers, called nodes, that agree on every update using shared rules instead of a central keeper.

Key term

Blockchain

One type of DLT, where transactions are grouped into blocks and each block is locked to the one before it, forming a chain.

Every blockchain is a distributed ledger, but not every distributed ledger is a blockchain. Can you guess why? (Hint: the word "chain".)

The magic ingredient

Digital Fingerprints

How do you prove a record was never touched? Give it a fingerprint.

In the warm-up, your fingerprint rule was simple addition. Real blockchains use a cryptographic hash function, most famously SHA-256, used by Bitcoin. Feed it any text, a single word or a whole book, and it returns a fingerprint of exactly 64 characters.

Three properties make it powerful: the same input always gives the same fingerprint; changing even one character gives a completely different fingerprint; and you cannot work backwards from the fingerprint to the original text. Try it below. This is a real SHA-256, running in your browser.

The main event

The Chain: Try to Cheat It

Each block carries the fingerprint of the block before it. Change one, and the chain snaps.

A block contains three things: some transactions, the fingerprint of the previous block, and a number called the nonce. The block's own fingerprint is calculated from all three. Because every block includes the previous block's fingerprint, the blocks are chained together. Touch an old block, and its fingerprint changes, which breaks the link to the next block, and the next, and the next.

To make cheating even harder, a block is only valid if its fingerprint starts with a few zeros, here "000". Finding a nonce that produces such a fingerprint takes thousands of guesses. This guessing work is called mining, or Proof of Work.

Many copies, one truth

The Network

Even if you could re-mine every block, you would still have to fool everyone else.

Your re-mined chain lives only on your computer. Thousands of other nodes still hold the honest copy. When nodes disagree, they follow a consensus mechanism: shared rules for deciding which version is true. In simple terms, the majority wins. A cheater with one copy is simply outvoted and corrected.

Consensus 1

Proof of Work (PoW)

Nodes called miners compete to solve the "000" puzzle you saw. The winner adds the next block and earns a reward. Secure, but uses a lot of electricity. Used by Bitcoin.

Consensus 2

Proof of Stake (PoS)

Instead of burning electricity, validators lock up ("stake") their own coins as a security deposit. Cheat, and you lose your deposit. Ethereum switched from PoW to PoS in September 2022.

Sort it

Public, private or permissioned?

Not every blockchain is open to everyone. Read each situation and choose the right type.

Type 1

Public

Anyone can join, read and validate. No owner. Example: Bitcoin, Ethereum.

Type 2

Private

One organisation controls who can join. Fast, but trust returns to that organisation.

Type 3

Permissioned / consortium

A group of known organisations, such as several banks, run the nodes together. Example platform: Hyperledger Fabric.

5
From chain to coin

The Birth of a Coin

Why would strangers spend electricity guarding a ledger? Because the ledger pays them.

Mining costs real money: computers and electricity. So Bitcoin pays the miner who adds each new block with newly created bitcoin, called the block reward, plus the fees in that block. That is how every bitcoin in existence was born: not printed by a central bank, but issued by the code as payment for securing the ledger.

The code also limits supply. The block reward started at 50 bitcoin in 2009 and is cut in half roughly every four years, an event called the halving. Total supply can never exceed 21 million. Compare that with the rupee, whose supply the RBI manages according to the needs of the economy.

Bitcoin's block reward, halving by halving

Block reward in bitcoin per block. The current reward of 3.125 dates from the April 2024 halving.

So the coin exists to pay for the blockchain, and the blockchain exists to record the coin. In the next class we will meet thousands of other coins and tokens that are built on top of blockchains. Ask yourself: what is each one paying for?

The Blind Spot: Land Records on a Blockchain?

Land disputes are among the most common cases in Indian courts. Records are often paper-based, scattered across offices, and open to forgery. Several governments, in India and abroad, have explored putting land records on a blockchain. Your group plays one player in this decision.
