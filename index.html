2001 to 2006

The Party

Everyone believed one thing: house prices only go up.

After the dot-com bubble burst in 2000 and the attacks of 9/11, the US Federal Reserve slashed interest rates to revive the economy. By 2003 its policy rate was just 1%. Money became cheap, loans became easy, and Americans started buying houses like never before.

Tap the bubbles to burst them

As demand rose, house prices rose. As prices rose, banks felt safe lending more, because even if a borrower stopped paying, the bank could take the house and sell it for more. That logic only works if prices keep rising. Hold on to that thought.

US home prices, index with 2000 = 100. Approximate values following the S&P Case-Shiller national index trend: up about 80% to the 2006 peak, then down.

Soon banks ran out of "good" borrowers with steady jobs and clean credit histories. So they turned to subprime borrowers: people with weak credit histories, unstable incomes, or no proof of income at all. Some loans were nicknamed NINJA loans: No Income, No Job, no Assets.

Key term

Subprime borrower

A borrower with a high risk of default: poor repayment history, low or unverified income. "Prime" borrowers are the safe ones; "sub" prime means below that standard.

Key term

Teaser rate

A very low starting interest rate for the first two or three years of a loan, after which it "resets" to a much higher rate. Borrowers were told: don't worry, you can refinance before the reset.

2
The machine behind the madness

How One Loan Became a Global Product

Why would any bank give a loan it knew might never be repaid? Because it did not plan to keep it.

Earlier, a bank that gave you a home loan kept it for 20 years and collected your EMIs. If you defaulted, the bank lost money. So it checked you carefully. By 2005 that model had changed. Banks gave loans and sold them within weeks. This is called originate to distribute: create the loan, pass it on, and let someone else carry the risk.

The loan travels away from the family, and the risk travels with it. By the end, the people holding the risk have never met the borrower.

The mango box analogy: a fruit seller mixes rotten mangoes into boxes, labels them "Premium Alphonso", and gets a quality certificate from an inspector he himself pays. The boxes travel to shops across the country. When customers open them, every shop is in trouble, and nobody trusts any mango box again. Replace mangoes with home loans, the label with AAA, and the shops with banks around the world. That is 2008.

3
Where were the regulators?

The Referees Who Slept

A crisis this big needs more than greedy players. It needs referees who look away.

In a well-run financial system, three referees protect the public: rating agencies that judge risk, regulators that set rules, and laws that limit how much risk a bank can take. In the years before 2008, all three failed together.

Tap a referee to wake them up
Referee 1

Rating agencies

Moody's, S&P and Fitch were paid by the very banks whose products they rated. If one agency was strict, the bank went to another. Thousands of mortgage products were stamped AAA and later downgraded to junk.

Referee 2

Deregulation

In 1999 the US repealed key parts of the Glass-Steagall Act of 1933, which had separated ordinary banking from risky investment banking. In 2000, a new law kept derivatives like credit default swaps largely unregulated.

Referee 3

Leverage limits

In 2004 the US Securities and Exchange Commission relaxed capital rules for the largest investment banks. They borrowed more and more. By 2007, Lehman Brothers was carrying roughly 30 dollars of assets for every 1 dollar of its own capital.

4
2007 to 2009

The Crash

When house prices stopped rising, the whole tower fell, one floor at a time.

US house prices peaked in 2006. Teaser rates began resetting, EMIs jumped, and subprime borrowers started defaulting in large numbers. Suddenly nobody knew how much the AAA bonds sitting on bank balance sheets were really worth. And because nobody knew, banks stopped trusting each other. They stopped lending to each other overnight. Credit, the oxygen of the economy, was cut off.

5
Meanwhile, 13,000 km away

India, and a Stranger Called Satoshi

India's banks held almost none of the toxic bonds. India still got hurt. Why?

Indian banks had little exposure to American subprime products. One big reason was the RBI's conservative approach in the boom years: it had made banks set aside more capital against risky real estate and stock market lending. So India did not face a banking collapse. Instead, the crisis arrived through three other doors.

Door 1 · Stock market

Foreign investors ran home

Panicked foreign institutional investors (FIIs) sold Indian shares to cover losses at home. The Sensex fell from its peak of 20,873 on 8 January 2008 to below 9,000 by late October 2008.

Door 2 · The rupee

Everyone wanted dollars

FIIs converted rupees into dollars to leave, so the rupee fell from about 39 to about 50 per dollar by early 2009. The RBI sold dollars from its reserves to slow the fall.

Door 3 · The real economy

Orders dried up

Exports, IT services and remittances slowed. GDP growth fell from about 9% in 2007-08 to about 6.7% in 2008-09. The RBI cut interest rates sharply and pumped money into the system.

Then, a stranger posted a document

On 31 October 2008, six weeks after Lehman collapsed, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine-page paper titled Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System. Its idea: money that needs no bank, no central bank, and no bailout. Trust would come from mathematics and a public ledger, not from institutions.

On 3 January 2009, Satoshi created the very first block of Bitcoin, called the genesis block, and hid a newspaper headline inside it, from The Times of London that day. Watch the chain begin:

Your three questions

  1. The decision: What did your player do between 2006 and 2008, and why did it make sense to them at that time?
  2. The blind spot: What risk was your player not seeing, or choosing not to see? Name one decision that could have changed the ending.
  3. The DeFi question: If your player's job were done by a smart contract on a public blockchain, with every transaction visible, would the crash have been prevented, or would it have happened faster? Give one reason.

Whole-class debrief

  • Which player did the class blame most? Compare with your Blame Meter reports.
  • Was 2008 a failure of people, of incentives, or of rules? Can one exist without the others?
  • Trust in banks broke in 2008. Bitcoin replaced trust in people with trust in code. What new things must we now trust?
  • Indian banks survived because the RBI was strict. Should the RBI be equally strict with DeFi today?
